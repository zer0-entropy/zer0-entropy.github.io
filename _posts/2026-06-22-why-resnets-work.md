Why ResNets Work: Residual Connections, Vanishing Gradients, and the Degradation Problem

### The Problem ResNet Set Out to Solve
By 2015, the deep learning community had a working assumption: stack more layers, get more accuracy. VGG and GoogLeNet had pushed depth into the teens and twenties, and the trend looked monotonic. But He et al. ran a simple experiment that broke this assumption — they trained "plain" CNNs (no shortcut connections) of increasing depth on ImageNet and CIFAR-10, and found that beyond a certain point, adding more layers made training error go up, not down.
This is not overfitting — overfitting shows up as low training error and high test error. Here, the training error itself got worse as depth increased. The authors call this the degradation problem, and it's puzzling on its face: a deeper network should, in theory, be at least as good as a shallower one. You could always take the shallow network's layers, copy them, and set every additional layer to an identity mapping — the deeper network would then match the shallow one exactly. The fact that real optimizers couldn't even find this trivial solution meant the issue wasn't capacity. It was something about how gradient-based optimization behaves once a network gets stacked too deep. Solving that problem is what residual learning is for.

### Vanishing Gradients: The Backstory
To understand why deep stacks are hard to train, it helps to revisit the classic vanishing gradient story. Backpropagation computes gradients for early layers by chaining derivatives across every layer in between. If each of those local derivatives is small (a near-saturated sigmoid, or weights initialized too small), the chained product shrinks geometrically with depth — by the time the gradient signal reaches the first few layers, it's effectively zero, and those layers stop learning.
By the time ResNet was published, this specific failure mode had already been substantially tamed by two tools: normalized weight initialization (schemes like Glorot/Xavier initialization, designed so signal variance neither explodes nor vanishes across layers) and Batch Normalization, which re-centers and re-scales activations at every layer so gradients stay in a well-behaved range. Together, these let networks with several dozen layers converge end-to-end with plain SGD.
And yet the degradation problem persisted even in networks built with these safeguards. He et al. explicitly point out that this degradation is unlikely to be the classic vanishing-gradient story replaying itself, since gradients in their deep plain networks were still checked to be healthy in magnitude. The real culprit, they argue, is an optimization difficulty: when the layers you're adding need to approximate something close to an identity mapping, it turns out to be surprisingly hard for stacked nonlinear layers (conv → BatchNorm → ReLU, repeated) to learn that mapping through ordinary gradient descent. So the fix needed isn't "more normalization" — it's a change to what the layers are being asked to learn in the first place.

### Residual Connections: The Fix
The idea is simple to state. Instead of asking a block of layers to learn a direct mapping from input to output, ask it to learn the difference between them:
y = F(x, {Wᵢ}) + x
Here F is the stack of weight layers inside the block (conv → BN → ReLU → conv → BN, for the basic two-layer block used in ResNet-18/34), and x is passed forward unchanged via a shortcut connection and added to F's output before the final activation. If the best thing a block can do is "nothing," the network only has to push F(x) toward zero — a much easier target for gradient descent to find than threading an identity mapping through several nonlinear transforms.
There's a wrinkle: addition only works if x and F(x) have the same shape, but ResNet architectures deliberately change the number of channels and downsample spatial resolution between stages. When that happens, the shortcut becomes a projection:
y = F(x, {Wᵢ}) + Wₛx
where Wₛ is a 1×1 convolution (with the matching stride) whose only job is reshaping x into the right dimensionality — it carries no new representational burden, just dimension-matching. For deeper variants (ResNet-50/101/152), each block itself is restructured into a three-layer bottleneck: a 1×1 conv that reduces the channel dimension, a 3×3 conv that does the actual spatial computation on this slimmer representation, and a final 1×1 conv that expands the channels back up before the shortcut addition. This keeps parameter count and compute manageable even as depth grows into the hundreds of layers; ResNet-18 and ResNet-34, being shallower, skip the bottleneck and use the plain two-layer block directly.
This structure also has a direct gradient-flow benefit, on top of the optimization argument above: differentiating y = F(x) + x with respect to x gives a term that includes a clean identity (derivative of x is 1), alongside whatever gradient flows backward through F. That identity term is parameter-free and can't vanish or saturate — it's a direct path for gradient signal back to earlier layers, regardless of how poorly-conditioned F's own gradient might be at extreme depth.

### Why ResNets Work
The paper's evidence for this is concrete and comparative. A plain 34-layer network had higher training error than a plain 18-layer network — the degradation problem in action. Swap in residual connections, and the relationship flips: the residual 34-layer network outperforms the residual 18-layer network on both training and validation error. Depth starts paying off again, exactly because the reformulation made "doing nothing extra" the easy default rather than a hard-won solution.
This let the authors push depth much further than was previously practical — successfully training networks up to 152 layers on ImageNet, and up to 1202 layers in CIFAR-10 experiments, without the same breakdown plain networks show at far shallower depths. The headline result: an ensemble of residual networks (anchored by the 152-layer model) reached 3.57% top-5 error on the ImageNet test set, taking 1st place in the ILSVRC 2015 classification competition — while still being less computationally complex than the much shallower VGG networks it surpassed.
The throughline across all of this: vanishing gradients motivated better initialization and BatchNorm, which solved that problem but exposed a second, subtler one — stacked nonlinear layers struggle to approximate identity mappings, which degrades very deep plain networks. Residual connections solve this second problem directly, by making identity the default the network falls back to, while incidentally also giving gradients an unobstructed path backward through the shortcut. That combination — easier optimization plus better gradient flow — is why depth became an asset again instead of a liability.
